How this course is designed

The adult-learning rationale for the Education Research Skills platform, CTLT edition.

Version 0.1, September 2026.

The answer in short

Teach lecturers the way the evidence says adults and academics learn.

  1. Start from their own problem, not from a topic.
  2. Show a worked example before asking them to produce anything.
  3. Make every check a low-stakes retrieval with feedback, and show them their own calibration.
  4. End every module with a piece of their own study written down and exportable.
  5. Wrap the self-paced modules in a human community with a real project running through it.

The platform carries the first four. CTLT's existing structures carry the fifth: learning communities, teaching enhancement grants, consultations, and the Asian Journal of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning. The platform should feed those, not replace them.

This course's own framework

A course about frameworks that has none of its own is not worth much. Design principle 10 below says why this page exists. Here is the course's own answer to the two questions modules 3.1 and 3.2 ask of everybody else.

The theoretical framework

Cognitive load theory. The constructs are intrinsic, extraneous and germane load. The prediction is that worked examples plus faded practice will raise learning gain more than exposition will.

The conceptual argument

NUS lecturers are asked to do education research, are rarely trained for it, have little time, and already run the classrooms the research needs. A short, case-based, self-paced course that ends in a proposal fits that setting.

That is module 3.1 and module 3.2 applied to the course itself. It also gives you a second worked example, written out where you can check it.

Who the learners are

Lecturers are adult professionals and experts in their own field. They are novices only in education research. Six facts about them drive the design.

Ten design principles, and where each shows up on the page

1. Start from the learner's problem

What the evidence says. Adults learn when the learning answers a need they already feel (Knowles). Instruction works best when it is anchored to a real problem (Merrill 2002). Relevant content is one of the eight features of effective faculty development (Steinert et al. 2016).

On the page. Step 1, "Why this matters", is one paragraph about the consequence for the learner's own paper or grant, using the words a reviewer would use. The "Your study" thread gives every module a job to do for the learner's own project.

2. Activate and calibrate before teaching

What the evidence says. Activating existing knowledge is Merrill's second principle. Practice testing is one of the two highest-utility techniques in the Dunlosky et al. 2013 review. Asking for confidence alongside an answer turns a quiz into a calibration exercise: the learner finds out not just what they got wrong, but where they were sure and wrong.

On the page. Step 2, "Where you stand", is four items, and each one is a bet rather than a bare confidence choice. Sure wins or loses 3 points, fairly sure 2, guessing 1. No answers are shown yet, and the same items return in step 7. Making confidence cost something is what turns the calibration line at the end from a remark into a result.

3. Explain plainly, one idea at a time, with a memory hook

What the evidence says. Working memory is limited, and instruction that overloads it fails (Sweller et al. 2019). Definitions that arrive with an example and a handle are retained better than definitions alone.

On the page. Step 3 has three short beats, one per term, each with a one-line memory hook ("a theory travels", "a theoretical framework is a theory you have signed up to", "a conceptual framework is your case for the study"). One "watch out" callout names the trap, and no more.

4. Show a worked example before asking for production, then fade

What the evidence says. Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving the same problems unaided; as expertise grows the effect reverses and problem solving wins (Sweller et al. 2019). A plain "now write your own" is worse for beginners.

On the page. Step 4 works Case A in three reveals. The learner predicts before each reveal, which keeps the example from being passive reading. Step 5 then gives twelve cards to classify with feedback. Step 6 asks for the learner's own case. The support fades across the three steps.

5. Practise with contrast, and give feedback specific to the error

What the evidence says. Interleaved practice and elaborative interrogation ("why is this one different?") carry moderate utility (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Feedback that names the specific confusion behind a wrong answer does more than "incorrect, try again".

On the page. The card sort mixes theories, adopted frameworks, conceptual arguments, and four deliberate traps (Kirkpatrick's levels, Miller's pyramid, Bloom's taxonomy, a literature review headed "Framework"). Every wrong placement gets feedback written for that placement. A fourth bin, "Neither", makes "this is a classification, not a theory" a claim the learner must commit to. In module 3.2, the lens table asks the learner to guess what each lens measures and what goes dark before the row is revealed.

6. Make them produce something they will reuse

What the evidence says. Adults are motivated internally and by usefulness (Knowles). Educational projects that participants carry through a programme are a feature of effective faculty development (Steinert et al. 2016). Integration into the learner's own world is Merrill's fifth principle.

On the page. Step 6, "Your study", has sentence starters, autosaves, and exports as text. The hub collects every module's output into one download. By the end of Part 3 the learner has a draft framework section for a real proposal.

7. Test again, show the gain, and show calibration

What the evidence says. Retrieval after instruction consolidates it (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Most online continuing education stops at knowledge and confidence; behaviour is rarely measured (Cope and Campbell 2026). Deciding the evaluation level up front is the fix.

On the page. Step 7 repeats the four items and reports the score before and after, the normalised gain, the points you staked before and after, and a table holding each stake to the hit rate it claims. Across the course this produces a learning-gain measure per module, the same instrument the bridging-course platform uses with students, and a calibration trace alongside it.

8. Keep it short, self-paced and stand-alone, but sequenced

What the evidence says. Health professionals prefer stand-alone self-paced modules with short readings and quizzes used for consolidation rather than gatekeeping (Samuel et al. 2025). Distributed practice beats massed practice (Dunlosky et al. 2013).

On the page. Modules are about 25 minutes. Steps are never locked, and neither are modules. This is a deliberate difference from the student-facing bridging course, where the essential modules unlock in order. Adults choose their order. The hub still shows a recommended sequence.

9. Build the community around the platform, not into it

What the evidence says. Intentional community building, longitudinal design and institutional support are three of the eight features of effective faculty development (Steinert et al. 2016). The programme community and the workplace community both matter (O'Sullivan and Irby 2011). Educator presence and a peer community are the main enablers of online engagement (Chambers 2025).

On the page. The hub names the human layer and links to it. The platform does not try to be a discussion forum. See the delivery model below for what wraps it.

10. Practise what it teaches, and say so

What the evidence says. This is a design choice rather than a finding. Lecturers are experts in their own teaching, and they notice when a course about frameworks has none. Naming the course's own framework respects that expertise and gives them a second worked example.

On the page. The "How this course is designed" page states the course's own theoretical framework (cognitive load theory, with intrinsic, extraneous and germane load as the constructs, and the prediction that worked examples plus faded practice will raise learning gain more than exposition) and its conceptual argument (NUS lecturers are asked to do education research, are rarely trained for it, have little time, and already run the classrooms the research needs; a short, case-based, self-paced course that ends in a proposal fits that setting). That is module 3.1 and 3.2 applied to the course itself.

Delivery model: a blended wrapper around the self-paced spine

The platform is the spine. The wrapper is what turns a course into a programme, and it maps onto what CTLT already runs.

  1. A cohort of 12 to 20 lecturers, 12 weeks. Lecturers work through the modules alone, about two a week.
  2. A learning community meeting of 90 minutes every three weeks, four in total. Each meeting is built around the "Your study" outputs of the modules just completed. Lecturers read one another's framework paragraphs and lens tables. The facilitator does not lecture.
  3. A named CTLT consultant for each cohort, and a peer pair for each lecturer.
  4. A capstone: a two-page study proposal (question, framework section, design and measures, ethics plan). Peer-reviewed in the last meeting and reviewed by the consultant.
  5. A pathway out: the proposal goes to a teaching enhancement grant application, an ethics application, or an AJSoTL or Teaching Connections submission.

This is the shape of CTLT's existing Professional Development Programme (Teaching): a faculty-mentored community that meets periodically and ends in a written artefact. The platform makes the between-meeting work concrete and measurable.

Evaluation plan, decided up front

Cope and Campbell 2026 found that thirty studies of asynchronous online education almost all stopped at knowledge and confidence. The fix is to name the level before launch.

The four Kirkpatrick levels, the measure for each, and where it comes from
Level Measure Source
Reaction Module rating; muddiest point themes Every module, on the page
Learning Score before and after; normalised gain; calibration Every module, on the page
Behaviour Proposals drafted; ethics applications submitted; grant applications; manuscripts submitted, within twelve months Capstone and a twelve-month follow-up
Results Funded projects; publications; teaching changes reported by the department Twelve-month follow-up

With consent, the platform's own telemetry becomes a scholarship-of-teaching study of the course. That is the same move the bridging platform makes, and it is the evidence CTLT and the NUS Educator Track both ask for.

What changed in September 2026, and why

Two changes, both from the same finding: the course was well written and still read as dry, because it was shaped like a document rather than an experience.

The course map became the grant form

The hub used to be a table of contents: fourteen cards, eleven of them marked "Planned". It answered the question "what is in this course" and never answered the one a busy lecturer actually asks, which is "what do I have at the end that I do not have now".

It is now the NUS Teaching Enhancement Grant application itself, rendered as a document, with the learner's own sentences in it. Every module writes one named box. Empty boxes show ruled lines and the module that fills them. The whole form is about 1,400 words of prose, and saying that out loud is the point: it is finishable.

The dashboard also carries the panel's weighting, because the form contains a trap. Significance is worth 40 per cent, more than the other three criteria together, and the form gives it no box of its own. It is spread across Scope and Aims, one line inside the Rationale, and Sharing. A lecturer left alone will under-write the highest-scoring criterion. So every box on the form says which criteria it feeds, and the ones feeding Significance say so in a heavier chip, at the point where the writing actually happens.

Ethics is shown as a second, separate document. The grant form has no ethics section, because ethics happens after the award. Folding modules 5.1 and 5.2 into the proposal would have made them look like a detour; they are not.

Eight steps became eight screens

Every step had the same shape, eight times in one scroll: label, time, heading, two paragraphs, widget. Consistency was the intention and flatness was the result. Each step is now one screen, with a progress strip above and Back and Next below, and the address bar keeps your place.

There is always a way out. A "Read as one page" button restores the old scroll and remembers the choice. The Open University surveyed 362 students on click-and-reveal activities of exactly the kind this course uses and found roughly a quarter actively disliked them, because they break reading flow and the revealed answer rarely matches what the learner was actually thinking. Gating without an escape hatch is how you lose people.

Pictures, at last

The platform had eleven interaction types and not one of them contained a picture. Every widget was made of text: cards of text sorted into bins of text, text you diagnose, text you reveal. That is a great deal of clicking and nothing to look at.

Eight visual widgets were added: a figure, a video that loads only when you ask for it, an artefact you mark up, sliders that rewrite a sentence and tell you what each setting cost, pieces of a study you put in order, a dilemma with consequences, an anonymous poll for questions that have no answer, and a hundred dots that turn statistical power into something you feel rather than calculate.

Two rules govern all of them. Not everything needs to be interactive: text is still best for abstraction, diagrams for relationships, interactives for processes and systems. And nothing on this platform says "Correct". Every success message carries something the learner did not already know, because for an audience of academics, praise reads as condescension.

Motion, kept on a short leash

The one animation that carries information is the step transition, where the slide direction tells you which way you moved. It lasts under a fifth of a second. There is no bounce and no overshoot anywhere, because springy motion reads as juvenile to professors. Anyone whose system asks for reduced motion gets none of it, and every animated figure ships a still image for them, since an animated GIF will otherwise play regardless of that setting.

See it working

Three modules are built. They are the design above turned into pages. Two of them, 3.1 and 3.2, follow the eight-step pattern. The third, 4.3, deliberately does not: finding what is wrong with a study is a judgement made under scarce attention, not a body of knowledge you can be tested on before and after, so it is written as one continuous case with a budget of four probes and no checks at either end. The pattern is a default, not a cage, and the break is there to show that.
