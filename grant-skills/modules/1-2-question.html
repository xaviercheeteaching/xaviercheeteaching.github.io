Module 1.2 of Part 1

Turning a hunch into a question

From "my students seem to struggle with this" to a question you can answer in one semester.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 2 minutes

Why this matters

The whole Teaching Enhancement Grant application is about 1,400 words of prose. The Project Rationale gets 400 of them and has to carry four separate things. Project Scope and Aims gets 200 and has to carry three more, including your module code and how many students are in the class.

Most of what sinks a proposal is decided here, before any method has been chosen. A question too big to answer in the time. A question too small for anyone to care. Or a hunch that was never turned into a question at all, so the method section is answering something nobody asked.

This module writes five of the boxes on that form. It is the heaviest single module in the course for that reason.

What you will be able to do

Turn a teaching hunch into one research question you can answer in a semester, say what you expect it to produce, and fill in the title, question, outcomes, aims and context boxes of the real application.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. The same four come back at step 7, and you get the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 7 minutes

The idea

A hunch is not a question

A hunch names a feeling: they struggle with this, they are not reading, the tutorials feel flat. A question names what you will find out and what would count as having found it.

Three moves get you from one to the other. Say what you can actually see, in the plainest words you have. Say what would count as evidence that it changed. Then bound it to one semester and one class you control.

Memory hook: a hunch describes you. A question describes what would settle it.

Three kinds of question, and a fourth

Pat Hutchings sorted scholarship of teaching questions into kinds, and the sorting is still the fastest way to work out what you are asking.

  • What is happening? Describes. What do students actually do when they attempt this? Cheapest to run, and the honest first study when you do not yet know what the problem is.
  • What works? Compares. Does this change move that outcome? The kind a funding panel expects, and the kind that needs a comparison you can defend.
  • What could be? Tries something with no local precedent and looks hard at what happens. Highest risk, and the one that needs the most careful measurement, because there is nothing to compare it with.
  • Theory building. Builds a new way of framing the problem rather than testing an existing one. Rare in a first project, and Part 3 of this course is about it.

Naming your kind early saves an argument later. A "what works" question that has never been asked what is happening usually turns out to be fixing something that was never diagnosed.

The 400-word box has four jobs

The Project Rationale is capped at 400 words and has to carry all four of these:

  • the teaching problem, positioned in higher education research (Module 1.1);
  • the research question (this module);
  • the anticipated educational outcomes (this module);
  • a literature review connected to theory (Modules 2.3 and 3.4).

Four things, 400 words, so about 100 words each. The literature review will want more than its share. This is why the question has to be one sentence: everything you spend on it comes out of the review.

The 200-word box wants numbers

Project Scope and Aims is capped at 200 words. Besides what the project sets out to do, the call asks for the module code, the current class size, and how the module is delivered.

Those three facts are the cheapest marks on the form and proposals lose them every year. A panel that cannot tell whether you teach 30 students or 300 cannot judge your design, your sample, your budget or your claim.

Where the 40% actually lives

The panel scores four criteria. Significance is the largest by a wide margin, and it is the only one with no box of its own.

Panel criteria, their weights, and which sections of the form they are scored in
CriterionWeightWhere it is scored
Research Problem20%Project Rationale, Project Scope and Aims
Literature Review20%Project Rationale
Method20%Approach and Implementation, Evaluation Plan and Method
Significance40%No box of its own. Project Scope and Aims, the outcomes line in the Rationale, and Sharing

Read that last row again. The heaviest criterion on the form is scored on sentences spread across three sections, none of which is labelled with its name. Write those three as administration and you have thrown away 40% of the marks while filling every box correctly.

Watch out

The outcomes line is where Significance is won or lost, and it is also where the call rejects outright. Outcomes that are only student perceptions or satisfaction are not enough. Something in that sentence has to be a thing students do rather than a thing students say about themselves.

Practice: scope it, then order it

Two exercises. The first has no right answer and is about what each choice costs. The second has one right answer and is about what has to come before what.

Step 6

About 8 minutes

Your study

Five boxes, and all five go straight onto the real form. What you write here is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it appears in the draft proposal on the course map as you write it.

Write prose you would submit, not notes to yourself. The word counts on the form are tight enough that a rough version is not much use later.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.

The NUS part

Local specifics, which change

Everything above this box is general. This bit is NUS, and it is the part to check before you plan anything around it.

  • The grant. Teaching Enhancement Grant, Learning Improvement Projects stream. In the FY2025 cycle the call went out in February, proposals were due in late March, and awards were notified in June. Your question has to be answerable in a semester that starts after June.
  • Ethics. Education research at NUS goes to LACE, the Learning and Analytics Committee on Ethics, run by CTLT and ALSET. It does not go to NUS-IRB. There is no retrospective review, so approval has to be in hand before the first piece of data exists.
  • Who to ask. CTLT runs the call. Your department's teaching lead will know your CTLT contact. For anything about this course, write to Dr Xavier Chee, xchee-01@nus.edu.sg.

Dates and stream names are from the FY2025 cycle. Read the current call before you rely on any of them.
