Module 4.1 of Part 4

Designs that fit a classroom

You teach every section. There is no control group, no second cohort and no ethical way to hold students back. There is still a design, and choosing it well is most of your funding case.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 1 minute

Why this matters

Almost every lecturer who wants to study their own teaching hits the same wall in the same week. You teach the only section. You cannot split the class and give half of them the teaching you believe is worse. The randomised trial you read about in the methods textbook is not available to you, and it never will be.

The usual response is to give up on comparison and describe what happened. That is what makes a proposal look thin. The better response is to notice that a design is not a trial: it is whatever lets you say the change, and not something else, produced the difference. There are six or seven ways to get that in a real module, and most of them cost nothing.

There is money in this too. The TEG panel weights Method at 20 per cent and Significance at 40 per cent. Significance asks how the project will change teaching practice, which is a claim about classrooms other than yours. A study that shows only that something happened once, in your class, with nothing to compare it against, cannot support that claim. Your design is where most of the Significance case is quietly won or lost.

What you will be able to do

Choose a design that fits a class you cannot randomise, say what it rules out and what it does not, and write it into the 200-word Approach and Implementation box.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. You will meet the same four questions again at step 7, and you will see the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 6 minutes

The idea

The comparison is the design

Strip away the vocabulary and every design is an answer to one question: compared with what? The rest is detail about timing and paperwork. When a reviewer says a study has no design, what they almost always mean is that it has no comparison, so any number of explanations fit the result equally well.

Memory hook: a design is not a procedure. It is a set of rival explanations you have arranged to rule out.

The rungs you can actually reach

These are ordered by what they rule out, weakest first. Every one of them has been used in published classroom research.

1. One group, measured at the end. You taught it, you gave a test, the marks were decent. This rules out nothing at all. It cannot even show change, because there is no earlier number to change from.

2. One group, measured before and after. Now you can show change. What you cannot show is that your teaching caused it. Students get better over a semester whatever you do, they get better at the test by having seen the test, and the weakest ones drop out along the way and stop dragging the mean down.

3. Two intact groups, one taught the new way. The non-equivalent groups design. The gain is a comparison running at the same time, so anything that hit the whole cohort that semester hits both groups. The cost is in the name: the groups were never equivalent. Students choose their tutorial slot, and the ones who take the 9am group differ from the ones who take the 4pm group.

4. Everyone gets both, in a different order. A crossover, or a switching replication. Half the class meets the new method on topics 1 to 3 and the old one on topics 4 to 6; the other half gets the reverse. Nobody is denied anything, which removes the ethical objection and most of the political one. Each student is compared with themselves, which removes every difference between students that you never measured. What it costs is that the topics have to be comparable, and that what students learn in the first half may carry into the second.

5. Several cohorts, before and after. An interrupted time series. Four years of marks on the same question, then the change, then two more years. This turns "they were improving anyway" from an accusation into something you can look at, because you can see the trend the change had to beat. It is the design most lecturers already have the data for and never think to use.

6. Randomise something you do control. You cannot randomise students. You can very often randomise which topic gets which treatment, which week the change starts, which of two equivalent worked examples a tutorial group is given, or the order in which each student meets the two versions. Randomising the schedule is not as strong as randomising the people, and it is enormously stronger than nothing.

Two short videos, if the vocabulary is new

Both are from the Research Methods and Statistics course at the University of Amsterdam. They are narrated slides rather than a person talking to camera, so they are quick and you can skim the visual and still follow. The first is the one to watch if you only watch one.

Ranking the rungs is a trap

A stronger design you cannot run is worth less than a weaker one you finish. The question is never "which design is best" in the abstract. It is: which rival explanation will a reader raise first, and does my design remove it?

If the obvious objection to your study is that this year's students were simply stronger, a crossover removes it and a bigger sample does not. If the obvious objection is that marks were rising anyway, five years of prior data removes it and a second tutorial group does not. Pick the rung that answers the objection you will actually get.

The design is also the Significance case

Method is worth 20 per cent to the panel. Significance is worth 40, and the application form never gives it a box. It is carried by Scope and Aims, by one bullet inside the Rationale, and by Sharing, and it is made believable by the design.

Significance asks how the project will change teaching practice. That is a claim about classrooms other than yours. A colleague in another department reading your result has to be able to think: if I did that, something like this would happen to me. A design with no comparison gives them no reason to think it, however good the teaching was.

Watch out

Writing "a quasi-experimental design was used" is not having a design. The words that carry meaning are: what varied, who it varied between, when you measured, and what the difference is being compared against. If a reader can answer those four from your Approach box, you have a design. If they cannot, the label does not help you.

Step 5

About 7 minutes

Practice

Put the study in order

Six pieces of a classroom study, shuffled. None of them is wrong and none of them is optional, so the only thing being tested is whether you know what depends on what. Move them with the Up and Down buttons, then check.

The dilemma

Three decisions, taken from inside a real semester. There is no score and no feedback between them, because the point is that you commit before you can see what it costs. The replay at the end names what each choice protected and what it traded away.

Step 6

About 5 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. What you write here is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it carries forward to the other modules. The second box goes straight into Approach and Implementation, section 3c of the TEG form, so write it as you would write it there.

At NUS

Most NUS modules have one lecturer and no parallel section, so a comparison between different students is usually not available to you. What you can nearly always vary is order, topic or week. Start there.

Class size decides which rungs are reachable. A first-year module of 200 or more gives you the numbers for a between-groups comparison; a final-year elective of 25 does not, and there a within-student design or several cohorts is the only honest option. The form asks for your class size in Scope and Aims, so the panel will read your design against it.

The call explicitly asks you to account for existing local data. Module evaluations and past cohort marks are the obvious ones, and they are more useful as a baseline trend than as a control group: several previous years show what normal variation looks like, where one previous year just gives you a rival explanation.

Ethics goes to LACE, CTLT's own committee, and approval comes before any data is collected, including data you already hold. Build that into the timeline. A design that needs consent from students you are also grading has to be built for that from the start, which is module 5.2.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.
