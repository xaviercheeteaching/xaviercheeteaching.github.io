Module 1.1 of Part 1

What counts as education research

Good teaching, scholarly teaching, and a study. Three different things, and only the third one gets funded as research.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 2 minutes

Why this matters

Everyone who changes something in their teaching and watches what happens is doing something worth doing. Very little of it is research, and a panel can usually tell in the first paragraph.

The FY2025 Teaching Enhancement Grant call names four things it rejects outright. One of them is a proposal that is really about a piece of software, with the teaching argument bolted on afterwards. This module is about the line, and about writing the one paragraph that puts you on the right side of it.

What you will be able to do

Say whether a piece of work is good teaching, scholarly teaching or a study, spot a proposal that is technology-led, and write the teaching problem your own study starts from in the form the Project Rationale asks for.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. The same four come back at step 7, and you get the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 6 minutes

The idea

Three things sit on one line, and the differences between them are small and consequential.

Good teaching

You notice something is not working, you change it, and you judge the result by what you see in the room. Your judgement is the instrument. This is most of teaching and there is nothing wrong with it.

Memory hook: good teaching stops at the change.

Scholarly teaching

You read what other people have already found, you choose the change for a reason you could state out loud, and you check the result with something better than your impression. It is still your class, and the answer stays in it.

Memory hook: scholarly teaching brings evidence in.

Education research

Everything scholarly teaching does, plus three things. A question fixed before the data. Evidence planned in advance to answer that question and not some other one. And a claim made in public, positioned against what other people already found, so that someone who does not teach your module can use it.

Memory hook: research sends evidence back out.

Why the third thing is the expensive one

Fixing the question first is cheap and awkward. It means you cannot go looking for the comparison that happens to have worked out, which is exactly what makes the result worth something to a stranger.

Planning the evidence in advance is what most proposals get wrong. Exam marks already exist, so they are tempting, and an exam written to grade students is rarely sensitive to the one thing you changed.

Making the claim in public is what a grant is buying. A panel is not funding your class. It is funding a finding that other teachers can act on.

Positioned in higher education research

The Project Rationale is capped at 400 words and has to carry four things: the problem positioned in higher education research, the research question, the anticipated educational outcomes, and a literature review connected to theory. This module writes the first of those.

Positioned means someone else has already asked something close to your question, and you say where yours sits against theirs. Same problem, different students. Same problem, and their fix did not transfer. Same problem, and nobody has looked at it in a class this size. Any of those is a position. "Students in my module find this hard" on its own is not.

The panel weights are Research Problem 20%, Literature Review 20%, Method 20% and Significance 40%. An unpositioned problem loses marks in the first two before the method has been read.

Watch out

The call rejects technology-focused proposals with little pedagogy. The test is not whether technology appears. It is which one arrived first. If the tool was chosen and then a problem was found for it, the writing will show it: the product is described in the first sentence and the students appear in the fourth. Write the problem first, and let the tool be the answer to it.

Step 4

About 5 minutes

Worked example

One case, worked in full. Write your own answer first, then reveal. The writing is what makes this work, so do not skip it.

Step 5

About 7 minutes

Practice: find the tool-first proposal

A first draft of a Project Rationale, seven sentences long. Click any sentence that would make a panel call this technology-led rather than pedagogy-led. Wrong clicks cost nothing, and not every sentence here is a problem.

Step 6

About 6 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. What you write here is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it carries into the proposal you are assembling across this course. The first box goes straight into the Project Rationale, so write it as prose you would submit, not as notes.

If you do not yet know whose work your problem sits next to, write the problem anyway and leave a gap. Module 2.3 is where you fill it.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.

About 2 minutes

The NUS part

Local specifics, which change

Everything above this box is general. This bit is NUS, and it is the part to check before you plan anything around it.

  • The grant. Teaching Enhancement Grant, Learning Improvement Projects stream. In the FY2025 cycle the call went out in February, proposals were due in late March, and awards were notified in June. Work backwards from June: the semester you plan to run the study in has to start after it.
  • Ethics. Education research at NUS goes to LACE, the Learning and Analytics Committee on Ethics, run by CTLT and ALSET. It does not go to NUS-IRB. There is no retrospective review, so approval has to be in hand before the first piece of data exists.
  • Who to ask. CTLT runs the call. Your department's teaching lead will know your CTLT contact. For anything about this course, write to Dr Xavier Chee, xchee-01@nus.edu.sg.

Dates and stream names are from the FY2025 cycle. Read the current call before you rely on any of them.
