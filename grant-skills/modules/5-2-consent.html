Module 5.2 of Part 5

Consent when you are also the teacher

You hold power over these students and you are asking them for something. The form cannot fix that. The process can.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 3 minutes

Why this matters

A student reading your consent form is not reading it the way a stranger's participant would. They are working out, quickly and privately, what saying no costs them with the person who marks their work. Most of them will conclude it costs nothing. Some will not, and you will never find out which.

This is the part of ethics that is not paperwork. Everything else in an application can be got right by reading the rules carefully. This one cannot, because the problem is not in the document. It is in the room.

The good news is that it is fixable, and the fixes are cheap. They are just not the fixes people reach for first.

What you will be able to do

Design a consent process for your own class that a committee will accept and a student can actually refuse: who asks, when they ask, who sees the answer, and what you can honestly promise about anonymity.

Before we start

One question with no right answer. Commit first, then see where colleagues landed. The rest of the module does not settle it.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. You will meet the same four questions again at step 7, and you will see the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 5 minutes

The idea

The problem is the room, not the form

Every information sheet says participation is voluntary. It is the most-written sentence in research ethics and it is usually true. But a sentence cannot undo the fact that the person asking is the person grading, and students are extremely good at reading that situation without being told about it.

So stop trying to solve it with words. Voluntariness is a property of the situation the student is standing in. Change the situation.

Memory hook: you cannot write your way out of a power imbalance. You have to design your way out.

Three levers, and that is all there are

Who asks. Not you. A colleague who does not teach or mark this module, a teaching assistant with no marking role, or CTLT. The person handing out the form should be someone whose opinion of the student cannot appear on a transcript.

When you find out. Not until final marks are submitted. If the consent forms are sealed and held by someone else until then, the question of whether knowing affected your marking never arises, because you did not know.

Who sees the answer. Saying yes and saying no have to look identical from the outside. Everyone hands in the same sealed envelope. Everyone clicks through the same page. If declining requires a student to do something visible, such as staying behind, walking out, or not handing something in, then it is not private and the class knows.

Memory hook: someone else asks, you find out late, and no is invisible.

Opting out of the data is not opting out of the teaching

This is the line that makes refusal safe, and it is the one most often got wrong with the best intentions. If the intervention is worth studying, every student gets it. What a student declines is the use of their data. Nothing else moves: not the teaching, not the marking, not their access to the materials.

The temptation is to give a declining student something else to do, or to teach them the old way so that they are "not in the study". Both are a penalty dressed as a courtesy, and both wreck the design as well, because you have just built a comparison group out of the students who said no.

Anonymity and confidentiality are different promises

Anonymity means nobody, you included, can tell who a response came from. Confidentiality means you can tell and you will not.

In a tutorial group of twelve, in one department, taught by one person, anonymity is not deliverable. You will recognise the handwriting, the argument, the way somebody phrases things. Promising it anyway is not a harmless formality; it is a promise a student may rely on when deciding what to write.

So promise confidentiality, and then say concretely what you will do: no quotation that could identify anyone, nothing reported at the level of a single tutorial group, no cell in a table smaller than a stated number, and a named point after which withdrawal is no longer possible because the data can no longer be traced back.

What the sheet has to carry

The Participant Information Sheet and Consent Form is a real document with a required shape, and three things on it are routinely wrong in draft.

Your dual role has to be stated plainly: you are the lecturer and the examiner as well as the researcher. A student who discovers that later has been misled by omission.

The contact details must belong to the committee that actually approved the study. For a departmental study that is the DERC, not NUS-IRB. Templates circulate with the NUS-IRB address already filled in, and it gets copied.

And if grades are involved, the sheet must ask for them, not announce them. "Your examination results will be included in the analysis" is a statement. Category 1B needs a request, answered in advance.

Watch out

Kindness is where this goes wrong. Reassuring a student who declined that it really would not have mattered, reassigning their work to another marker so nobody can accuse you of anything, explaining in your methods section why the one non-participant was unusual: each of those is a decent instinct, and each one either re-asks a question the student already answered or tells the room who they are.

Step 4

About 4 minutes

Worked example

One case, worked in full. Write your own answer first, then reveal. The writing is what makes this work, so do not skip it.

Step 5

About 6 minutes

Practice

First: mark up a real sheet

Below is a draft Participant Information Sheet of the kind that gets sent to a committee every week. Five things in it are problems. Click any line that looks wrong to you. A wrong click costs nothing and tells you why the line is in fact fine, which is worth knowing too.

Then: a student says no

One decision at a time. You will not see what a choice costs until after you have made it, which is the honest version of how this actually happens.

Step 6

About 3 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. What you write here is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it carries forward to the other modules. The first box goes into your ethics application, so write it as you would write it there.

Write the process, not the intention. "Consent will be obtained voluntarily" is not a process. A named person, a named week, and a described mechanism is.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.
