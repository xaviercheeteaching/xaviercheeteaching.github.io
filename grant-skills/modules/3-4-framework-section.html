Module 3.4 of Part 3

Writing the framework section

You chose a lens. Now it has to become two or three paragraphs, inside a box that is already carrying three other jobs.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 1 minute

Why this matters

Modules 3.1 and 3.2 were about knowing which thing you have and what it lights up. This one is about writing it down, in the space you are actually given.

The FY2025 Teaching Enhancement Grant call gives you one Project Rationale box, capped at 400 words, and asks it to carry four things: the problem positioned in higher education research, the research question, the educational outcomes you anticipate, and a literature review connected to theory. Your framework is not a fifth item added to that list. It is what makes the fourth one count, and it has about a hundred words to do it in.

The gate this module clears

The call says it plainly: proposals that do not properly situate the project by means of a literature review and a theoretical framework will not be considered. Both, not either. This module writes the framework half. Module 2.3 writes the literature half. A proposal with one and not the other does not get read on its merits.

The two paragraphs also share one word budget, which is why they must not say the same thing twice.

What you will be able to do

Write the framework paragraphs your proposal needs: the mechanism you are claiming, how it applies to this module, what it makes measurable, and what it leaves dark.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. You will meet the same four questions again at step 7, and you will see the answers then.

These are about the writing, not about the distinction between the three terms. That was module 3.1, and it is assumed here.

Step 3

About 5 minutes

The idea

Three paragraphs, in this order

A framework section has a shape, and it is the same shape every time. First, the mechanism. What does this theory claim happens, stated generally, in two sentences, with your module not yet mentioned. Second, this setting. What that mechanism means here, for these students, under this assessment, in a way that could not be written about anybody else's course. Third, the consequence. One sentence saying what the lens makes visible and measurable, and one saying what it leaves dark.

Two paragraphs is fine if you are tight, and you will be. Four is a sign the second one is really a literature review.

Memory hook: mechanism, then this place, then what you will see and what you will not.

Named is not used

The commonest failure is a theory that appears once and is never heard from again. "This project is underpinned by constructivism" is a sentence that survives being deleted, which is the test. If the paragraphs after it would read the same without it, the theory is not framing anything.

A theory is being used when it makes a prediction that could turn out to be wrong. Write the sentence that starts "the framework predicts that", and if you cannot finish it, you have named a theory rather than adopted one.

Memory hook: if deleting the theory changes nothing, it was decoration.

The dark sentence is not a limitation

From module 3.2: every lens lights part of the field and leaves the rest dark. Where that goes on the page matters more than people expect. In the limitations section it reads as something a reviewer dragged out of you. In the framework, one sentence long, it reads as a choice, and it closes the question of why this framework and not another one before the question can be asked.

If you want it said again

Three and a half minutes, and it covers the distinction from module 3.1 in somebody else's words. Optional. Skip it if the distinction is already solid.

Watch out

The framework paragraph and the literature paragraph share 400 words with two other demands. If your framework paragraph starts summarising studies, it has stopped being a framework and started spending the literature review's words a second time.

Step 4

About 5 minutes

Worked example

The running case from modules 3.1 and 3.2, written out one paragraph at a time. Write your own version first, then reveal and compare. Your paragraph does not have to match; it has to do the same job.

Step 5

About 6 minutes

Practice: mark up a paragraph

Nine sentences from a draft framework paragraph. Click every sentence that is not doing a framework's job. Leave the ones that are. A wrong click costs nothing and tells you what the sentence was doing that you missed.

Watch out

The strongest sentences in that draft were already there. Most framework paragraphs are not short of good sentences. They are short of room, because the weak ones have taken it.

Step 6

About 5 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. The second box goes straight into the Project Rationale of your proposal, so write it in the words you would submit. Everything is saved on this device and nowhere else.

If you filled in module 3.1 or 3.2, the material is already there. This is where it becomes prose someone else can read.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.

Step 8

About 1 minute

Close

Muddiest point

Rate this module

Readings

The NUS part

AJSoTL, the Asian Journal of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning, is run by CTLT. It is open access, charges no fees, and publishes articles of 4,000 to 6,000 words with an abstract of at most 250 words, in APA style, through masked peer review.

At that length the framework has room to be a section of its own rather than a paragraph, but it sits in the same place and does the same job: early, before the method, because the method has to follow from it.
