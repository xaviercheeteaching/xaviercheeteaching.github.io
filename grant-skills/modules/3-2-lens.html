Module 3.2 of Part 3

What a lens shows and what it hides

Every framework lights one part of the field and leaves the rest dark. The skill is saying which part, on purpose, before a reviewer asks.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 1 minute

Why this matters

A reviewer who asks "why this framework and not another?" is not asking you to defend the theory. They are asking whether you know what your lens cannot see. If you can say what goes dark, and why you accepted that, the question is answered. If you cannot, the paper reads as if the framework was chosen after the results.

What you will be able to do

Predict which findings a given framework will make visible and which it will suppress, and name the one you can test with the access you actually have.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. You will meet the same four questions again at step 7, and you will see the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 5 minutes

The idea

A framework is a lamp

Bordage's image is the right one. A framework illuminates part of the field and magnifies it, which means the rest goes dark. That is a feature, not a fault. A study that claims to look at everything has chosen nothing.

The same lamp turned on the same room lights different things depending on where you stand. Two careful teams can study one intervention, choose different frameworks, and report different findings, without either of them making a mistake. The lens decided what counted as data before a single number was collected.

Memory hook: a lamp lights one part of the field, and the rest goes dark.

All four could be true

In step 4 you will look at one case through four lenses: cognitive load theory, cognitive offloading, self-regulated learning, and assessment-driven learning. Say this plainly to yourself before you start, because it is the point of the whole module. All four could be true at the same time. Choosing one is not a claim that the others are false.

It is a claim about which mechanism you can test properly with the access you have. You have the marks. Do you have the think-aloud recordings? The tool logs? The load ratings? The lens you can defend is the one your data can reach.

Memory hook: choosing a lens is a claim about your access, not about the other lenses being wrong.

The practical consequence

Two sentences belong in your framework section. The first says what this lens makes visible and how you will measure it. The second says what it leaves dark and why you accepted that. Written together they answer the reviewer's question before it is asked, and they show that the framework came before the results rather than after them.

Watch out

"What goes dark" is not a weakness to hide in the limitations section. It is the sentence that shows a reviewer you chose the lens on purpose.

Step 4

About 4 minutes

Worked example

One case, four lenses, one row at a time. The mechanism is given to you. You write what you would measure under that lens, and what the lens leaves in the dark. Then reveal, and say how close you were.

Case A

A first-year programming course gave every student an AI coding assistant for all assignments. Assignment marks rose. In the closed-book practical exam, where the assistant was withdrawn, marks fell below the previous cohort's, and students struggled most with debugging.

Step 5

About 6 minutes

Practice: two studies, two lenses

Two published studies of nearly the same thing. University students, a generative chatbot, one academic task, a matched comparison condition. What differs is the lens. That is what makes the pair worth reading side by side: the intervention is not doing the work, the framework is.

Read both, then write one sentence each way. One sentence is enough. A good sentence names something the other lens could not measure, and ties it to what that study actually did measure.

Watch out

Neither study is the better one. They asked different questions because they held up different lamps. If you had the log data but not the load ratings, only one of these studies was open to you.

Step 6

About 5 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. Pick two or three lenses that could plausibly explain what you are seeing, and fill in the same three columns for each. What you write is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it carries forward to the other modules.

Pick lenses that genuinely compete. Two lenses that agree with each other teach you nothing about what goes dark.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.
