Module 4.2 of Part 4

Measuring learning, not satisfaction

Asking students whether they found it useful is the easiest measure to collect and the one the funding call rules out first. This is what to collect instead, and how to check your study could see it.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 2 minutes

Why this matters

This module exists because of one sentence in the TEG call. Educational outcomes, it says, "should go beyond surveying students' perceptions or satisfaction levels". A few lines later it calls perception data "at most indirect" evidence.

That sentence is not a stylistic preference. It is the single commonest reason a well-intentioned teaching project fails to convince a panel, because a satisfaction survey is the cheapest thing in the world to run and the hardest thing to argue from. The call asks instead for "the type of evidence that will be collected to measure real learning enhancement in students' skills, attitudes, and/or behaviours", and it rejects proposals that "do not display rigour in terms of conceptualization, planning and collection of evidence".

None of that means student experience does not matter. It matters a great deal, and it is worth collecting. It just cannot be the thing your claim rests on, because a student can rate a module highly having learned very little, and can rate it poorly having learned a great deal. There is good evidence that the second of those is common in exactly the teaching that works best.

What you will be able to do

Tell a direct measure of learning from a perception measure dressed as one, choose measures of skills, attitudes or behaviours that fit your study, and check that your class is large enough for the measure to show anything.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. You will meet the same four questions again at step 7, and you will see the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 6 minutes

The idea

What a satisfaction measure actually records

A satisfaction item records how the experience felt to the student at the moment they filled in the form. That is real information and it is worth having. It is not information about what changed in the student.

The gap between the two is not small, and it does not point in a comforting direction. In a well-known physics experiment at Harvard, students in active classes learned measurably more than students in a matched lecture, and rated their own learning lower. Effortful teaching feels worse while it is happening. If you measure the feeling, you can end up preferring the teaching that works less well.

Memory hook: satisfaction measures the experience. Learning measures the student.

The same argument, about teaching evaluations

Philip Stark of Berkeley presenting his own paper on student evaluations of teaching. It is four minutes and it is the same argument you have just read, made about the numbers your department already collects on you.

Three places to look for a direct measure

The call names three kinds of outcome: skills, attitudes and behaviours. Use its own words, and look in all three, because the cheapest good measure is often not the one you first thought of.

Skills. A performance on a task the student could not do before, marked against a rubric that someone else could apply to the same script and reach the same mark. Not the module grade: a module grade contains six other things you did not change, and it is far too coarse to move.

Attitudes. A published scale with known properties, administered twice. Self-efficacy, interest, sense of belonging, tolerance of ambiguity. Not "did you find this useful", which is a satisfaction item wearing an attitude's coat. An attitude measure is a real outcome in its own right, and it is not a substitute for a skills measure.

Behaviours. What students actually did. Attempts made before asking for help, solutions revised rather than abandoned, whether the technique you taught shows up in a later assignment where nobody asked for it, whether they came to consultation hours. This is the class lecturers forget, and it is frequently the cheapest, because the submission system and the LMS have been recording it all along.

One test that catches most of the disguises

Ask of any proposed measure: could a student score higher on this without having learned anything?

If the answer is yes, it is not a learning measure. Self-rated confidence: yes, by enjoying the module. An assignment mark where the AI assistant was allowed: yes, by delegating better. Attendance: yes, by turning up. A survey run only at the end: yes, because there is no earlier number to have moved from.

Run the test the other way too. A measure that a student could not score higher on without having learned something is the measure you want, even if it is more trouble to collect.

Reliability and validity, one line each

Reliability is whether the measure gives you the same answer twice. Validity is whether it measures the thing you named. A measure can be perfectly reliable and measure the wrong thing entirely, and that is by far the more common failure in teaching research: a satisfaction survey is reliable, repeatable and consistent, and it is not a measure of learning.

Existing local data is not optional

The call asks you to discuss "any existing local data and how it influences the evaluation method adopted". Module evaluations, past cohort marks, question-level assessment data, LMS and submission logs. Two reasons it is worth doing properly.

First, it hands you a baseline you did not have to collect, and more importantly a picture of normal year-to-year variation. If the same question has moved by four marks between each of the last five cohorts, a four-mark improvement is not a finding.

Second, a proposal that ignores the data already sitting in the department reads as a plan made without looking. That is close to what the call means when it rejects work for lacking rigour in the planning and collection of evidence.

Watch out

Adding "before and after" to a perception item does not turn it into a learning measure. A confidence scale administered twice is a good attitude measure, measured properly, and it is still not evidence that the students can do anything they could not do before. Keep it, and put a skills or behaviour measure beside it.

Step 5

About 8 minutes

Practice

Find the satisfaction in disguise

A real-shaped Evaluation Plan and Method box, ten lines. Some of them are direct evidence that something changed in the students. Some of them are perception, or a mark too coarse to isolate anything, standing where the evidence should be.

Click every line that would not survive the panel's question: does this measure a change in the student? Wrong clicks cost you nothing, so click anything you are unsure about.

Could your study even see it?

A measure has a second job, and it is the one nobody checks. It has to be able to show the effect when the effect is really there.

Below are a hundred dots. Each one is a hypothetical repeat of your study, run in a world where your intervention genuinely works by the amount the second slider says. A filled dot is a repeat that would come out statistically significant. The sliders start where a great many NUS classroom studies start: a class of 40 split into two groups of 20, and a real but modest effect.

Sixteen dots. In a world where your teaching genuinely works, a study this size finds it about one time in six. The other five times you write up a null result, and the null result is really about your class size.

Now move the sliders and watch what it takes. To catch an effect of that size eight times in ten you need roughly 175 students in each group, which is 350 students, which is more than most NUS modules have. Push the second slider instead: at a large effect, twenty students per group finds it about seven times in ten.

Watch out

This is why the choice of measure and the size of your class are one decision, not two. A narrow measure of exactly the thing you changed shows a much larger effect than an overall module mark does, and a larger effect is one a small class can actually detect. Chasing a two-mark difference in a module grade with forty students is a study that cannot succeed however good the teaching is.

When you cannot get more students, and you usually cannot, there are three honest moves. Measure something closer to what you changed. Measure it more than once per student, so each student contributes more than one number. Or run it over more than one cohort and pool.

Step 6

About 5 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. What you write here is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it carries forward to the other modules. The second box goes into Evaluation Plan and Method, section 3d of the TEG form.

That box is capped at 300 words and you are not the only one writing into it. Module 4.3 adds the threat to your claim and module 6.1 adds the analysis. Aim for about 150 words here and leave them room.

At NUS

Module evaluations run every semester and the data for your module already exists. The call asks you to say what local data you hold and how it shapes your method, so a proposal that does not mention it reads as one written without looking.

Past cohort marks, question-level assessment data and LMS or submission logs are usually obtainable through your department or your learning technologist. Ask before you write the plan, not after: what you can get changes what is worth proposing.

Class size decides what you can detect. A large first-year module gives you the numbers for a modest effect; a final-year elective of 25 does not, and there a repeated within-student measure or two cohorts pooled is the only thing that will work.

Ethics goes to LACE, CTLT's own committee, and approval comes before any data is collected. That includes reusing marks and logs you already hold for a research purpose, which surprises people. Module 5.1 covers it.
