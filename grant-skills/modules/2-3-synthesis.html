Module 2.3 of Part 2

Synthesis, not summary

Fourteen studies listed one after another is not a literature review. A panel wants one claim, with the studies as evidence for it.

About 25 minutes. Eight steps. Everything you write stays on this device.

Step 1

About 1 minute

Why this matters

The FY2025 Teaching Enhancement Grant call gives you one box called Project Rationale, capped at 400 words, and asks it to carry four things at once: the problem positioned in higher education research, the research question, the educational outcomes you anticipate, and a literature review connected to theory. Four demands, four hundred words. About a hundred words each.

A hundred words is roughly six sentences. Six sentences cannot list fourteen studies, and a panel would not want them if they could. What six sentences can do is make a claim about what is known and name the thing that is not.

The gate this module clears

The call is blunt about it: proposals that do not properly situate the project by means of a literature review and a theoretical framework will not be considered. Both, not either. This module writes the literature half. Module 3.4 writes the framework half. Neither one alone gets you through.

Literature Review is also 20% of the panel's score, and the same 400 words are being read for three of the four criteria at once.

What you will be able to do

Turn a set of studies into one claim about what is known, and write the gap your study fills as something a panel can score.

Step 2

About 2 minutes

Where you stand

Four questions. This is not a grade. It is here so that you and we can see what the next twenty minutes actually add. You will meet the same four questions again at step 7, and you will see the answers then.

For each one, say how sure you are. Confidence that does not match accuracy is the useful signal, and you only get it if you answer honestly now.

Step 3

About 5 minutes

The idea

Summary is organised by study. Synthesis is organised by claim.

That is the whole distinction, and there is a test for it. Read the first three words of every sentence in your paragraph. If they are author names, you have written a summary. The order of the paragraph is then the order in which you happened to read the papers, which is an order no reader has any reason to care about.

In a synthesis the first three words are the claim. The studies arrive afterwards, in brackets, as the evidence for it. Three sentences of summary usually collapse into one sentence of synthesis, and the meaning gets sharper as the words go.

Memory hook: if the sentence starts with a name, it is a summary.

A gap is cut out of what is known

You cannot write the gap first. It only exists as a shape in something else: this is established, that is established, and the thing between them has never been shown. So the synthesis has to come first, and the gap is its last sentence.

Two gaps that are not gaps. "Nobody has studied this in Singapore" names a setting, not a question, and the panel's next thought is that maybe nobody needed to. "More research is needed" is true of everything ever published. A gap a panel can score says what the existing work could not show, and why your study can show it.

Memory hook: a gap has a shape only because something around it is solid.

Before you can synthesise

Most first drafts fail in the other direction from the one people expect. They do not have too many studies to organise. They have four, because education research hides in journals a discipline search never touches, and four studies will not support a claim about a field. Module 2.1 is where searching lives; this seven-minute video is here because a thin search is the most common reason a synthesis will not come together.

The practical consequence

Your literature paragraph has about six sentences to do three jobs. Say what the studies together show. Say where they disagree and what explains the disagreement. Say what none of them could show. Anything else in that paragraph is being paid for out of the words your research question needs.

Watch out

Satisfaction data is the quiet word-thief. A study reporting that students liked the sessions cannot be evidence for or against a claim about learning, so citing it costs you a sentence and buys nothing. If a study does not bear on your claim, it does not belong in a 400-word box, however carefully you read it.

Step 4

About 5 minutes

Worked example

One paragraph, worked in full. The same five studies come out the other end as a different paragraph. Write your own answer first, then reveal. The writing is what makes this work, so do not skip it.

Step 5

About 6 minutes

Practice: mark up a paragraph

Nine sentences from a draft rationale. Click every sentence that only describes. Leave the ones that argue. A wrong click costs nothing and tells you why the sentence is doing more than it looks.

Watch out

Cutting a study from the paragraph is not the same as not having read it. Reading forty and citing eight is normal. Citing all forty is how a panel learns that you could not decide what your claim was.

Step 6

About 5 minutes

Your study

Now your own project. What you write in the third box goes straight into the Project Rationale of your proposal, so write it as you would write it there. Everything is saved on this device and nowhere else, and it carries forward to the other modules.

Write the claims before the gap. If you find yourself writing the gap first, you are guessing at what is missing rather than reading it off what is there.

Step 7

About 2 minutes

Check again

The same four questions, in the same order. Answer them again, and say how sure you are again. This time you get the answers, your score before and after, and whether your confidence matched your accuracy.

Readings

The NUS part

AJSoTL, the Asian Journal of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning, is run by CTLT. It is open access, charges no fees, and publishes articles of 4,000 to 6,000 words with an abstract of at most 250 words, in APA style, through masked peer review.

Masked means the reviewer does not know it is you. Your literature paragraph has to carry the argument on its own, with no help from your name or your department.
